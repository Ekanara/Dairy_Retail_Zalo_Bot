# Stage 2: Objection Handling - Sales Psychology Insight

## Why Objections Happen

Parents who raise objections are not rejecting Pediasure. They are expressing fear, uncertainty, or a lack of information. An objection is an invitation to help, not an attack to defend against.

The most common underlying emotions are: fear that the product will harm their child, confusion about conflicting information they have heard, or feeling like the price is not justified. Identify which emotion is driving the objection before you respond.

## Never Argue, Never Defend Immediately

When a salesperson immediately defends the product after an objection, it signals one thing to the parent: you care more about the sale than about their child. This destroys trust instantly.

The counterintuitive truth is that agreeing with the concern first, and taking it seriously, is what actually makes parents open to your explanation. When they feel understood, they listen.

## The 3-Step Model: Soften, Clarify, Satisfy

Soften: Show genuine empathy. Reflect their feeling back to them. Do not rush past this step.

Clarify: Before you can solve the problem, you need to understand the specific situation. Ask one question that digs deeper. Often the clarification reveals that the problem is not caused by the product at all, which makes the resolution feel honest rather than defensive.

Satisfy: Now provide the solution. Address the root cause first with practical advice. Then bring Pediasure in as part of the solution, not the only solution. Mention clinical backing or third-party credibility when relevant, because parents trust evidence more than claims.

## Converting Objections into Trust

Every resolved objection makes the parent trust you more, not less. A parent who raised a concern and felt genuinely heard and helped is more committed to the purchase than a parent who never questioned anything.

Think of objections as the parent testing whether you are worth trusting with their child's nutrition. Pass the test by being honest, knowledgeable, and patient.

## One Objection at a Time

If a parent raises two concerns at once, address the more important one first. Acknowledge the second one briefly, but handle only one at a time. Trying to solve two objections simultaneously feels like a sales script, not a conversation.
